The young man became an owl

33 lines · from Melville Jacobs’s Coos texts, dictated by Annie Miner Peterson (1933–34); keyed 1986–1990.

1

tła-há·tssíya dí·lul

The young man became an owl

2

tłə-dí·lúł qłə́m-du kwi-yuxʷúm·ɛ,

The young man used to journey about in the night time,

3

aɴ-dú mínt'ci kwi·-dlú·g̣wa,

he was never once at home,

4

gu·s-míɴ-du·-kwí·-qłə́m yuxʷúm·ɛ.

at night he was always going about.

5

tsú-kwi·-hɛ́·wi,

Now he grew up,

6

tsú-húmstsəm.

and he married.

7

tsú-wɛ́n-i'ldᵘwa tłə-dəhú·mis,

Then he spoke in this manner to his wife,

8

tłgwə́ls-wantł-hu·t'súwa.

I will make a canoe.

9

tsú-á·yu kwi·-łú-dzí·-dzu·nis

So to be sure he was working at it.

10

mín·t'ci tłə-dəhú·mis də'mítłgwə́la wɛ́n-íldᵘwa,

One time he spoke thus to his wife's younger brother,

11

kwítc-dí·lúł

Young man you

12

kwa-'nðtsí·nł-łá·dza

go get my adze

13

áyu-łá-tłə-dí·lúł,

Of course the young man went,

14

tsú-hantł-níya-tłə-dətsí·nł.

and then he was going to give him his adze.

15

hɛⁱ-má·tsi-g̣úʻ-kwi·-dlú·g̣wa'itc,

Now he was sitting high up above (and he said),

16

dí·lúł

Young man

17

łá·dza kwə-'nðtsí·nł

Get me my adze

18

wɛ́·n-kwi·-wá'-nu.

That is the way he was doing.

19

dí·-kwə-nðtsí·nł

Here is your adze!

20

aɴ-dú-gwadá·'nu,

He did not even notice,

21

má·tsi-dáʻ-wɛ́·n-wá'nu,

he merely continued to do like that,

22

dí·lúł dí·łúł

(hooting) Young man! young man!

23

kwə-'nðtsí·nł-łá·dza

get me my adze!

24

tsú-wəs·í-duhwíyɛt tłə-dí·lúł.

Then the young man ran back home.

25

má·-tsi-g̣úʻ-kwi-dlú·g̣wa,

He just sits high up above,

26

hɛ́l·ɛgwətc kwi·-dlú·g̣wa.

he is sitting (up) on a limb of the tree.

27

wí-aɴ-wə́-x̣-djíʻ g̣ɛ́ʻ-djiʻ.

I could not get up there.

28

wɛ́n-k̓wɛ·nɛ́·nu.

That is what he informed her about.

29

wɛ́n-kwi·-g̣wá-'niya-łá tłə-t'ccí·miłədjə

Then he went on higher up on the tree.

30

wí-dí·łɛ́ⁱ dɛ'ngi-qłəm ma·-dú-akʷ'ái tłə-dətsí·nł.

And now today every night he is still wanting his adze.

31

x̣-wɛ́ntc má·tsiyɛ-qłə́m kwi-ak'ál tła-dətsí·nł.

That is why he still calls out for his adze at nights.

32

wí-áiwa qłə́mniyu-k̓a kwi·-qa'wá-ya ak'ál tłə-dətsí·nł.

The next people still hear him crying out for his adze.

33

tsú-tsi wɛ́·s kwə-'nəbá·saq̓

Now that is all of my myth.

The voice in these texts is Annie Miner Peterson’s, recorded by Melville Jacobs in 1933–34.
