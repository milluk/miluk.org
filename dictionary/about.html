About the dictionary

This dictionary was compiled between 1986 and 1990 from the Miluk texts Melville Jacobs recorded from Annie Miner Peterson in 1933 and 1934. It then sat for thirty-six years in a word processor format no current program opens, written in an alphabet I built for a machine that no longer exists. What follows is that work, recovered and reset. My original introduction begins after this note.

What was recovered

All 684 entry files came back intact. From them this edition carries 1,111 headwords, 4,167 attested Miluk forms, and 1,277 English senses. Each entry prints its attestations in full: the sentence in which the word occurs, its translation, and the story and line where Jacobs recorded it. There are 2,678 such quotations here, and every one has been checked to contain the word it illustrates.

Behind the book sits the corpus itself, 151 texts and 7,723 numbered lines, with every dictionary entry pointing at the lines that attest it and every line pointing back at the entries it contains.

The writing system

In 1986 the concordance program held 64 characters to a reference and the keyboard offered no phonetic symbols. I built a substitute alphabet from punctuation: a colon for length, a less-than sign for stress, a number sign for barred l, an exclamation point for glottalization. It served the analysis it was built for. It is not what Jacobs wrote, and it is not readable by anyone who does not already know the key.

This edition returns the text to Jacobs's own transcription, as he set it out in the phonetics section of Coos Narrative and Ethnologic Texts (1939). Where he prints tc, dj, tł and t'c, this edition prints them. His statement governs: each of those characters represents a phoneme, and they are not spellings to be simplified. The small capitals ɴ, ʟ and ᴍ are his, for the shortened sonancy he describes in final position. So are the raised dot for length, the dot below for the velars, and the under-mark for the anterior palatals that carry, in his words, a distinct y infection.

One difference from Jacobs is deliberate. He marks stress with a raised prime after the vowel. This edition places an acute accent on the vowel itself. The reason is alphabetization: a free-standing mark counts as a letter to a sorting program, which scatters a word and its stressed form to different parts of the list, and it also obscures the stem when forms are gathered under one head. Jacobs was setting type for a book of texts. A dictionary has to be searched. A reader comparing this edition against the 1939 volume should expect that difference and no other.

What was corrected

Keying several thousand lines of phonetic text by hand, on a keyboard that had none of its characters, produced differences between the transcription and the printed source. Every Miluk line of the 1939 volume has now been read against the transcription, line by line.

That collation found 922 differences. The largest single class, 341 of them, is a hyphen standing where Jacobs prints a raised dot: the two keys sit beside one another, and the same file often has the word both ways. Those have been corrected here where the reading is unambiguous. The remainder, including a class of l and ł confusions that the available scan cannot settle, are recorded rather than changed, and the list travels with this edition.

What is unfinished

Of the 4,167 forms, 110 are not attested anywhere in the digitized corpus. They are marked in the text so a reader can see them for what they are.

The 1939 volume is collated. Its companion, Coos Myth Texts (1940), is not, and the myths are the larger part of the corpus.

The 1990 work was itself unfinished, and this edition preserves that rather than papering over it. My introduction describes gathering every form of a word under one head. Roughly four hundred entries here still stand alone, most of them from the letter files where words waited to be filed. Sorting them is morphology, not transcription, and it is the next work rather than a defect in this one.

Sources and method

The language in this book is Annie Miner Peterson's. She was among the last people to speak Miluk, and she dictated these texts in her seventies, translating them back herself. The recording is Melville Jacobs's, made in 1933 and 1934 and published by the University of Washington in 1939 and 1940. What I have done is take the texts apart into their morphemes and put them back together as a lexicon.

The recovery, the conversion, the collation against Jacobs and the typesetting of this edition were carried out in 2026 with the assistance of Claude, an AI system made by Anthropic. Every correction it proposed was checked against the printed source before it was applied, and the ones that could not be checked were left alone and listed.

Introduction (1990)

The following is a concordanced Miluk word list taken mostly from Melville Jacobs' Coos Myth and Ethnologic Texts. While Melville Jacobs did extensive work on the language of Miluk, he did not to morphemically analyze the texts he had recorded. All his texts are provided with translation, but only phrase-for-phrase translations and not morpheme-for-morpheme translations. The following is an attempt to breakdown his texts and various others' texts into their morphemic constituents.

Finding the available texts, most of which have been unused for generations, has been a problem. Having done the necessary bibliographic and search work, the problem of trying to organize such a large and diverse set of texts was itself a burden. Not only were the texts diverse, but some of the texts themselves were quite questionable, as to the situation of the informant and to the ability of the field worker. Some of the texts were so dubious that I chose not to use them in my word list.

For example, a number of texts were recorded by John P. Harrington that are listed under the subheading Miluk, but the informants for Harrington were not necessarily Miluk speakers. Coquille Thompson, a native Athapaskan speaker, was one of Miluk's informants. The Athapaskan family is entirely unrelated to the Penutian family, in which one finds Miluk. Throughout Thompson's records, one will find the two languages mixed to such an extent to make them rather useless. Most people of Thompson's day would speak more than two languages and so separating one language from the other, after 20 years of disuse, must have been difficult apparently.

Another main problem with a number of the texts were that the people recording "Miluk" were not trained to transcribe what they heard. One such list came from the U.S. Army. A total lack of glottal consonants and other known Miluk sounds are completely missing from their records.

One of the other problems with some of the older works was that there appear to have been different provincial dialects scattered over a small area. The records of Gatschet and Dorsey seem to confirm this, but what remains to be researched is how these dialects compare with the dialect of Annie Miner Peterson, Melville Jacobs' informant.

It was for these reasons that I choose to use Jacobs almost exclusively. Annie Miner Peterson had Miluk "fresh" in her mind, as opposed to Coquille Thompson, and she also was of one particular dialect group (Annie mentions her distaste for another provincialism in the "Origin of Death" story's footnotes). This made for very consistent patterns in her speech. The process then of breaking phrases down to a morphemic level, was that much simpler.

The next hurdle for analyzing such a corpus of texts and their multifarious transcriptions was time. Time was the one thing the project would require the most. Entering in the hundreds of pages of texts would not be a week's work. Fortunately, however, my advisor, professor James Fox, supported me completely on my work and through his constant support I am able to present this dictionary.

After entering in the texts I then numbered all the texts so that the lines would match phrase for phrase. This took quite a while because at first I was unfamiliar with Miluk and matching phrases up with their English translations was very difficult. Then after numbering all of the phrases and the stories to which they belonged, the resulting phrase list was concordanced by Word Cruncher, a concordancing program.

The results was that I could now view a single Miluk word like `yeklu' dog and find every example of it in the compiled corpus. Thus making the job of defining a word much simpler. A list of fifty phrases that contained yeklu with English `dog' in each translation made it easier to pinpoint the meaning of the word than scattered occurrences in the texts.

After pinpointing nouns, verbs, and adjectives (even though translations were and are not exact) the rest of the search was for the multiple grammatical morphemes unique to this language or language family. This has been the most formidable task, since the grammatical morphemes are completely foreign to an English speaker and while many of the uses of some are very idiosyncratic with regards to their environment. There is a large corpus of texts, but the corpus is not large enough to make idiosyncratic clitics readily definable. We will always miss the insights of native speakers as to the semantic effects of these morphemes.

After giving rough translations to all the individual morphemes my last task so far has been to arrange them under one word. To use an English analogy, instead of having dog, dogs, dogged, hang-dog, hot dog, dog-eared spread out throughout the list, I shortened the list to have just the word dog with all the other references on or pertaining to dog under that heading -- dog. So all the allomorphs for dog and their Miluk counterparts, I now have under the morpheme dog and allomorphs for the Miluk word for dog yeklu, I now have under the morpheme yeklu.

The voice in these texts is Annie Miner Peterson’s, recorded by Melville Jacobs in 1933–34.

Miluk is the heritage of the Coquille Indian Tribe, the Confederated Tribes of Coos, Lower Umpqua and Siuslaw Indians, and the Confederated Tribes of Siletz Indians.
