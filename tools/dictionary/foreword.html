This dictionary was compiled between 1986 and 1990 from the Miluk texts Melville Jacobs recorded from Annie Miner Peterson in 1933 and 1934. It then sat for thirty-six years in a word processor format no current program opens, written in an alphabet I built for a machine that no longer exists. What follows is that work, recovered and reset. My original introduction begins after this note.

What was recovered

All 684 entry files were recovered and are now preserved byte for byte with a hash manifest. The restored edition carries 1,111 headwords and 4,167 Miluk forms. The 1990 files supply the entries, forms, glosses, examples, and historical citations. In 2026 those documentary values were parsed into structured records, the 1986 keyboard notation was converted for display, source links and verification flags were derived, and static pages were generated. Those later operations are recorded separately; they are not represented as work already present in 1990.

Entry pages expand at most eight quotations. Additional resolved citations are linked or summarized, citations to material outside this edition are counted separately, and unresolved 1990 citations remain unresolved. Thus all attestations travel in the data and navigation even when they are not all printed in full on one page.

Jacobs’s two University of Washington volumes contain 111 Miluk-bearing published texts: 77 in 1939 and 34 in 1940. The recovered public corpus contains 108 records and 7,149 numbered lines representing 110 of those texts. The 1939 page heading “A man obtains fir power” (printed pages 28–29; listed in the contents as “A man obtained fir power”) is absorbed at lines 69–90 of the Hadjíyasa record. “The water got high” (printed pages 58–59) is absorbed at lines 92–118 of the He eats human children record. The 1940 text “The rock point person lost his good luck thing” (printed pages 133–135) is absent from the recovered corpus. Splitting the absorbed texts or importing the absent text is pending source-integrity work requiring Troy’s authorization. The complete title-to-record inventory records a required starting-page locator for every text, an optional verified complete page span, and distinct contents and heading title fields in tools/dictionary/provenance/publication-inventory.json.

The earlier 151-record working dataset also contained 35 Jacobs slip-file records (574 lines) and eight empty section containers. Those records are preserved in the repository as outside-edition material but are not loaded or published as this edition’s corpus. Numerical-section classification establishes the boundary of that recovered export; it does not by itself prove that every published text was recovered.

The writing system

In 1986 the concordance program held 64 characters to a reference and the keyboard offered no phonetic symbols. I built a substitute alphabet from punctuation: a colon for length, a less-than sign for stress, a number sign for barred l, an exclamation point for glottalization. It served the analysis it was built for. It is not what Jacobs wrote, and it is not readable by anyone who does not already know the key.

This edition returns the text to Jacobs's own transcription, as he set it out in the phonetics section of Coos Narrative and Ethnologic Texts (1939). Where he prints tc, dj, tł and t'c, this edition prints them. His statement governs: each of those characters represents a phoneme, and they are not spellings to be simplified. The small capitals ɴ, ʟ and ᴍ are his, for the shortened sonancy he describes in final position. So are the raised dot for length, the dot below for the velars, and the under-mark for the anterior palatals that carry, in his words, a distinct y infection.

One display difference from Jacobs is deliberate. He marks stress with a raised prime after the vowel. This edition places an acute accent on the vowel itself. The reason is alphabetization: a free-standing mark counts as a letter to a sorting program, which scatters a word and its stressed form to different parts of the list, and it also obscures the stem when forms are gathered under one head. Jacobs was setting type for a book of texts. A dictionary has to be searched. Other intentional restoration changes are identified in the correction and transformation records rather than being silently folded into the documentary values.

What was corrected

Keying several thousand lines of phonetic text by hand, on a keyboard that had none of its characters, produced differences between the transcription and the printed source. The 2026 collation covers the stated portions of the 1939 volume; its own notes exclude printed pages 112–114.

That collation recorded 922 observations. The largest single class, 341 of them, is a hyphen standing where Jacobs prints a raised dot: the two keys sit beside one another, and the same file often has the word both ways. The workbook labels 343 observations as applied, representing 337 unique applied transformations; 579 observations remain unresolved. The complete machine-readable record, including original values, dispositions, reasons, and verification pages, is at data/correction-ledger.json.

What is unfinished

Of the 4,167 forms, 110 are not attested anywhere in the digitized corpus. They are marked in the text so a reader can see them for what they are.

The stated portions of the 1939 volume are collated, subject to the coverage gap described above. Its companion, Coos Myth Texts (1940), is not, and the myths are the larger part of the corpus.

The 1990 work was itself unfinished, and this edition preserves that rather than papering over it. My introduction describes gathering every form of a word under one head. Roughly four hundred entries here still stand alone, most of them from the letter files where words waited to be filed. Sorting them is morphology, not transcription, and it is the next work rather than a defect in this one.

Sources and method

The language in this book is Annie Miner Peterson's. She was among the last people to speak Miluk, and she dictated these texts in her seventies, translating them back herself. The recording is Melville Jacobs's, made in 1933 and 1934 and published by the University of Washington in 1939 and 1940. What I have done is take the texts apart into their morphemes and put them back together as a lexicon.

The recovery, conversion, collation against Jacobs, and typesetting were carried out in 2026 with AI assistance. The repository preserves the complete 922-observation ledger, the separate effective-diff receipt for later presentation and field repairs, and the original values needed to reverse those changes. The 1940 volume and later scholarship, including Anthony P. Grant’s paper on John Milhau’s 1856 Hanis vocabularies, were not used to alter this 1990/Jacobs edition.
